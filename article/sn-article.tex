\section{Methodology} \label{sec:Methodology}


In this work, we investigate the multiscale dependence structure of financial
time series by analysing how volatility components interact across temporal
scales. Our approach is conceptually motivated by the cascade picture of
financial dynamics introduced by Arnéodo, Muzy and Sornette
\cite{arneodo1998}, where information and volatility propagate from coarse to
fine scales in a structured, directional manner.  
In analogy with the multiplicative cascade models used in turbulence and
financial time series, we treat the observed log–price fluctuations as the
result of interacting scale components whose dependence structure can be
revealed through a combination of wavelet decomposition and mutual–information
analysis.

The methodology consists of four main steps, each serving a distinct purpose in
capturing the cascade–like organisation of volatility:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Wavelet decomposition of log–price dynamics.}  
    Following the spirit of multifractal cascade models, we first decompose the
    log–price series into fluctuations at different temporal scales.  
    A smoothed Haar wavelet is used to isolate local increments at scale $a$,
    producing a multiresolution representation analogous to the scale–indexed
    increments in multiplicative cascade models.

    \item \textbf{Construction of multiscale log–volatility fields.}  
    In cascade theory, the amplitude of fluctuations at each scale plays a
    central role.  
    To capture this, we compute a local energy measure (rolling RMS) of the
    wavelet coefficients and take its logarithm, yielding a log–volatility field
    $\ell_a(t)$ that reflects the instantaneous strength of fluctuations at
    scale $a$.

    \item \textbf{Mutual–information maps across scales and time lags.}  
    The cascade picture predicts that fluctuations at one scale influence those
    at neighbouring scales with a characteristic time delay.  
    To quantify this, we compute the mutual information between log–volatility
    components at different scales and time shifts.  
    The resulting mutual information map (MI map) $I(a,\tau)$ reveals the dominant lagged interactions
    between scales, analogous to the “direct cascade” structure described in
    \cite{arneodo1998}.

    \item \textbf{Extraction of ridge descriptors.}  
    The MI map typically contains a ridge representing the most probable lag
    $\tau$ at which scale $a$ interacts with the reference scale.  
    By fitting an asymmetric Gaussian profile to each scale row, we extract
    descriptors such as the ridge center $\mu(a)$, left and right widths
    $\sigma_{\mathrm{L}}(a)$ and $\sigma_{\mathrm{R}}(a)$, and amplitude $A(a)$.  
    These descriptors provide a compact summary of the cross–scale causal
    structure, analogous to the scale–dependent parameters in multiplicative
    cascade models.
\end{enumerate}

This conceptual methodology mirrors the structure of our previous work on
Castaing distributions and multiplicative processes, where the goal was to
characterise the statistical organisation of fluctuations across scales.  
Here, instead of modelling the distribution of increments directly, we analyse
the geometry of the MI ridge, which plays an analogous role: it encodes how
volatility propagates across scales and time, revealing the underlying cascade
mechanism.

The implementation details, numerical procedures, and stability considerations
are presented separately in Section~\ref{sec:technical_details}.

\subsection{Ridge--descriptor behaviour across scales}

To analyse the cross--scale dependence structure, we first examined the 
\textit{mean descriptor curves with variance} for both assets. This representation 
highlights systematic differences between BTC and the S\&P500. In particular, the 
\textbf{ridge centre} for BTC shifts progressively toward \textbf{positive lags} once the 
scale exceeds approximately $10^{2}$. This indicates that large--scale volatility 
components in BTC tend to exert their influence further into the future compared 
to the corresponding behaviour observed in the S\&P500.

A similar forward--directed tendency is visible in the right--hand width parameter 
$\sigma_{\mathrm{right}}$. For BTC, $\sigma_{\mathrm{right}}$ increases more rapidly and extends 
further toward positive lags, implying a longer persistence of the cascade 
interaction in the direction of future time. Together, these observations suggest 
that the BTC cascade exhibits a more pronounced forward--propagating structure, 
consistent across batches and robust with respect to the variance of the 
descriptor estimates.

\section{Technical Details}\label{sec:technical_details}

In this section, we present the detailed numerical procedures used to implement
the methodology described above. The structure follows the practical workflow
of the analysis, similar in spirit to the step–by–step procedure adopted in our
previous work on estimating the Castaing parameter $\lambda$.

\subsection{Data Handling and Preprocessing}

1. \textbf{Batching.}  
   The data is then divided into batches of fixed length ($30{,}000$ samples), of 2‑minute observations. Due to difference in trading window for historical $S\&P500$ and full day trading for $BTC$ this window correspond respectively to around 7 months and 1 month.

\subsection{Wavelet Transform and Detail Extraction}

1. \textbf{Wavelet construction.}  
   A continuous Haar wavelet is generated using a differentiable smooth–step
   function. This ensures numerical stability during FFT–based convolution.

2. \textbf{Scale grid.}  
   Scales are logarithmically spaced between $4$ and $2000$, producing $25$
   wavelet detail series per batch.

3. \textbf{FFT convolution.}  
   The continuous wavelet transform is computed using FFT–based convolution%
   \footnote{FFT convolution implemented using \texttt{scipy.signal.fftconvolve}.},
   which significantly accelerates computation for long time series.

\subsection{Local Volatility Estimation}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Rolling RMS.}  
   For each scale, local volatility is estimated using a rolling window of
   length $50$, 
\[
   \sigma_a(t) = \sqrt{\frac{1}{50}\sum_{k=t-49}^{t} W(a,k)^2},
   \]
    computed using a rolling window implementation%
   \footnote{Rolling RMS computed using \texttt{pandas.Series.rolling}.}.
    \item \textbf{Log–volatility.}  
   A small constant $\varepsilon = 10^{-12}$ is added before taking the
   logarithm to avoid numerical issues.
\end{enumerate}
   
\subsection{Mutual–Information Map}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Centering.}  
   Each log–volatility series is centered to remove scale–dependent offsets.
   \item \textbf{MI estimation.}  
   Mutual information between scale $a$ and the reference scale
   $a_{\mathrm{ref}}$ is computed for lags $\tau \in [-400,400]$ using a
   nonparametric estimator%
   \footnote{Mutual information estimated using \texttt{sklearn.feature\_selection.mutual\_info\_regression}.}.
   \item \textbf{MI map.}  
   The result is a matrix of size $(25,801)$ for each batch.
\end{enumerate}


\subsection{Lag–Window Restriction}

To improve numerical stability during ridge fitting, the MI map is restricted
to a central lag window (typically $|\tau| \le 300$).  
This removes flat tails that contain no meaningful structure but can distort
the fitting procedure.

\subsection{Baseline Correction and Normalization}

Before fitting, each MI row is processed as follows:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Baseline subtraction.}  
   The 10th percentile of the row is subtracted to remove background bias.
   \item \textbf{Normalization.}  
   The row is scaled to the interval $[0,1]$ to ensure stable optimization
   across scales.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Asymmetric Gaussian Fitting}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Model.}  
   Each MI row is fitted with an asymmetric Gaussian,
   

\[
   g(\tau) = A \exp\!\left[-\frac{1}{2}
   \left(\frac{\tau - \mu}{\sigma(\tau)}\right)^2\right],
   \qquad
   \sigma(\tau) =
   \begin{cases}
   \sigma_{\mathrm{L}}, & \tau < \mu,\\
   \sigma_{\mathrm{R}}, & \tau \ge \mu,
   \end{cases}
   \]


   using nonlinear least squares%
   \footnote{Fitting performed using the \texttt{lmfit.Model} interface.}.
   \item \textbf{Warm–start strategy.}  
   Fits proceed from coarse to fine scales.  
   The parameter estimates from scale $a_{i+1}$ are used as initial values for
   scale $a_i$, improving convergence and reducing sensitivity to noise.

   \item \textbf{Reset rule.}  
   After five consecutive failed fits, warm–start parameters are reset to
   default values to prevent propagation of poor estimates.

\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Quality Control}

A fit is rejected if any of the following conditions are met:

\begin{itemize}
    \item amplitude $A < 0.05$ (ridge too weak),
    \item $\sigma_{\mathrm{L}}$ or $\sigma_{\mathrm{R}}$ reaches its upper bound,
    \item center $\mu$ lies outside the lag domain,
    \item reduced chi–square $\chi^2_{\mathrm{red}} > 1$,
    \item numerical failure of the optimizer.
\end{itemize}
Rejected fits are marked and excluded from subsequent averaging.

\subsection{Descriptor Assembly and Averaging}

For each accepted fit, the following descriptors are recorded:


\[
\mu(a), \qquad \sigma_{\mathrm{L}}(a), \qquad \sigma_{\mathrm{R}}(a), \qquad A(a).
\]



To assess robustness, the entire pipeline is repeated across multiple batches.
For each descriptor $d(a)$, the empirical mean and variance are computed to assess general properties of the timeseries.
